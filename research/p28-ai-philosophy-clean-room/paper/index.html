When Variation Replaces Its Target

Identity Constraints on Causal and Counterfactual Comparison

P28 case studyEnglish case page
Status: independent AI-authored clean-room research artifact. It was not submitted to, accepted by, peer reviewed by, or endorsed by the 2026 AI Philosophy Competition.
1 When Variation Replaces Its Target: Identity Constraints on Causal and Counterfactual Comparison Abstract Difference-making reasoning asks what happens when a target is changed, removed, or replaced. Yet some interventions and counterfactuals alter conditions that partly individuate the target itself, creating an ambiguity about what contrast the evidence represents. I argue for a target-individuation constraint: causal and counterfactual claims about X require an independently warranted interpretation of whether the compared conditions represent within-X variation, X versus absence of X, or X versus a replacement X*. Formal continuity of a model variable cannot settle this issue, because notation can group distinct worldly properties under one symbol. The central requirement is therefore contrast matching between target individuation, the represented comparison, and the causal conclusion. This yields a limited metaphilosophical consequence. Philosophical case variation incurs an identity burden only when a modified case is used to infer a further property of a supposedly persisting target. If persistence itself is under test, or the comparison is explicitly presence versus absence, persistence need not be assumed. 1. Variation Requires the Right Contrast A familiar form of reasoning asks what difference something makes. We vary a feature, remove it, or replace it, compare outcomes, and infer what follows from the contrast. Causal intervention makes this structure especially explicit: alter X, hold relevant background conditions fixed, and determine whether Y changes. Counterfactual reasoning generalizes the pattern: ask what would happen if X, or some condition involving X, were different. This strategy contains an interpretive requirement that can be easy to overlook. Before an outcome difference can support a specific causal claim about X, we need a defensible account of what contrast involving X the compared conditions represent. They may represent two values of X, two realizations of X, the presence and absence of X, or X and a distinct replacement X*. These are all legitimate forms of comparison. They do not, however, support the same conclusions. The difficulty becomes especially visible when the identity of a target depends partly on background conditions that the comparison itself changes. Institutional powers offer a simple example. A veto is not merely an act called “refusal.” It is a rule-governed power in virtue of which an authorized refusal blocks, suspends, or otherwise prevents an institutional outcome. Alter the relevant rule structure far enough and several possibilities arise. The same veto may persist with a narrower scope. The veto may be realized differently. The veto may disappear altogether. Or another institutional power may replace it. Suppose we ask how veto authority affects the rate at which proposals are adopted. One reform restricts the veto to a smaller class of proposals. Another replaces handwritten veto notices with authenticated electronic notices. A third removes the blocking force of refusal, leaving only a power to trigger reconsideration. A fourth simply abolishes the officeholder’s negative power, leaving no substitute at all. Each comparison may genuinely concern veto authority, but in different ways. The first plausibly compares two values or scopes of the same power. The second compares two realizations. The third2 compares veto authority with a different institutional power. The fourth compares the presence of veto authority with its absence. If proposal adoption rises after the third reform, the evidence may support: replacing veto authority with a reconsideration power increases proposal adoption. It does not automatically support: weakening veto authority increases proposal adoption. If adoption rises after the fourth reform, the evidence may support: possessing veto authority rather than lacking it reduces proposal adoption. Again, that does not automatically show what happens when veto authority is weakened while remaining instantiated. The central problem is therefore not that every comparison must preserve an instantiated target. A legitimate causal comparison may concern X precisely by comparing its presence with its absence. The problem is instead whether the interpretation of the contrast matches the conclusion drawn from it. I defend the following thesis: Target-Individuation Thesis: A difference-making inference concerning X requires an independently warranted interpretation under which the compared conditions bear to X the contrastive relation stated or presupposed in the conclusion. Formal variable continuity alone cannot determine whether the relevant contrast is within-X variation, X/no-X, or X/X*. This thesis develops a concern with cross-world identity that has important antecedents. Pust (2001) and Matthen (1999) connect explanatory comparison with questions about individuation and cross-world identity in debates over selection and individuals. Helgeson (2015) is the closest antecedent to the present argument: he argues explicitly that identity assumptions matter symmetrically across competing explanatory positions and cannot be secured merely by reusing the same name or symbol. Relatedly, Franklin-Hall (2016) and Woodward (2016) show that the scientific appropriateness of variables is not settled merely by their formal availability. The contribution here is therefore not the bare claim that identity matters, nor the bare distinction between variation and replacement. The aim is to organize these concerns around contrast matching and to distinguish three inferential roles that target identity can play: testing whether a target persists, comparing its presence with its absence, and assuming persistence as a bridge premise in order to infer some further property. 2. Property Identity and Property Instantiation The first distinction is between the identity of the represented target and whether that target is instantiated in each condition. Suppose X is veto authority. A comparison can remain specifically about X even when one condition lacks any veto authority. A study comparing institutions with veto authority to institutions without it may legitimately answer a question about what difference possessing veto authority makes.3 The second condition does not need to instantiate X. What must remain fixed is the interpretation of the target and the contrast: one condition contains X; the other is an absence condition relative to X. This shows why “target preservation” must be stated carefully. If preservation is taken to mean that every compared condition instantiates X, it is too strong. The relevant continuity is continuity of the represented target of inquiry, not universal instantiation of that target. The contrast can take several forms: 1. X1 / X2 : one value or state of X versus another; 2. R1 /R2 : one realization of X versus another; 3. X /no- X: presence versus absence of X; 4. X/X*: the target versus a distinct replacement. Each may be methodologically legitimate. The difference lies in what proposition it can support. An X /no- X comparison may establish that possessing X, rather than lacking X, affects Y . It does not by itself establish that increasing or decreasing X within its range affects Y . An X/X* comparison may establish that replacing X with X* affects Y . It does not by itself establish a withinX effect. A realization comparison may show that different implementations preserve or alter an outcome while X remains fixed at the relevant level. This distinction also prevents an important mistake about constitutive dependence. Suppose background condition B partly constitutes X. Removing B may simply eliminate X. Nothing further need replace it. In such a case, the comparison need not be misdescribed as X/X*. It may be a perfectly good X /no- X contrast. The philosophical burden arises when evidence generated by one contrast is redescribed as evidence for another. If removal of B eliminates veto authority, then the resulting evidence may bear on the effect of having veto authority rather than lacking it. It cannot automatically be redescribed as evidence about weakening veto authority while it persists. Likewise, if alteration of B creates a reconsideration power, the resulting evidence may bear on the effect of replacing veto authority with that power, but not automatically on the effect of varying veto strength within a persisting kind. The governing principle is therefore contrast matching: the individuation of the target, the represented comparison, and the causal conclusion must line up. 3. Constitution and the Three Ways a Target Can Change Some targets are partly individuated by constitutive background conditions. To possess a legal entitlement, hold an office, cast a vote, or exercise a veto depends on more than intrinsic physical features. Functional and relational properties can similarly depend on wider structures for what they are. This matters because altering constitutive conditions can generate different kinds of contrast. First, there can be a value change. The target remains instantiated, but some parameter or domain changes. Restricting veto authority from all proposals to a defined subset may alter its scope while preserving the relevant institutional power.4 Second, there can be a realization change. The target remains the same at the relevant level while its implementation changes. Replacing a paper veto notice with an authenticated electronic instruction may preserve the same rule-governed blocking relation. Third, there can be elimination or replacement. Removing the relevant constitutive relation may leave no instance of X, generating an X /no- X contrast. Alternatively, the changed structure may instantiate a different property X*, producing an X/X* contrast. These possibilities can be observationally similar with respect to an outcome Y . Proposal adoption may rise whether the veto is narrowed, abolished, or replaced by a weaker but categorically different institutional power. The outcome difference therefore cannot, by itself, determine which contrast occurred. This is the key underdetermination. Difference-making evidence tells us that compared conditions differ in their consequences. It does not by itself determine whether the difference is best interpreted as within-target variation, target absence, or target replacement. Nor does constitution collapse into causation here. If B partly constitutes X, that does not mean B causes X. Constitution instead constrains how a change in B should be interpreted. A causal analysis may manipulate B, but before drawing a conclusion about “changing X,” it must determine whether the resulting condition represents a different state of X, the absence of X, or a different property. The contrastive interpretation is therefore part of determining the content of the causal proposition. It need not always be settled in advance in a strict temporal or methodological order; modelling, individuation, and explanatory purpose may interact. The stronger point is that the causal equations alone do not determine which worldly contrast is being represented. 4. The Individuation Consistency Condition The hard problem is now clear. What is required to distinguish legitimate within-X, X /no- X, and X/X* contrasts without pretending to solve the entire metaphysics of identity? Any inquiry about the causal or counterfactual role of X already relies, explicitly or implicitly, on some basis for identifying X. Otherwise the variable has no determinate worldly target. That basis may be functional, relational, institutional, structural, conventional, or partly interest-relative. The present argument does not privilege one metaphysics of properties. What it requires is a weaker condition: Individuation Consistency Condition: A difference-making comparison concerning X must employ an independently warranted individuation of X sufficient to determine what contrast the compared conditions represent—such as X1 / X2 , X /no- X, or X/X*—and the conclusion must match that contrast. Several points follow. First, the condition does not require X to be instantiated in every condition. If one condition satisfies the individuation basis for X and another does not, the latter may simply be an absence condition. The comparison can still genuinely concern X.5 Second, multiple realization is fully compatible with the condition. If paper and electronic procedures instantiate the same institutional role under the relevant individuation basis, then a realization change need not create either absence or replacement. The issue is not sameness of implementation but which relation to X each condition bears. Third, the basis must be independently warranted in the sense relevant to the inference. Suppose veto authority and reconsideration authority both reduce proposal adoption. If the causal question is whether veto authority reduces adoption, we cannot establish that reconsideration authority is merely another value of veto authority solely because it produces a similar outcome. That would allow the observed effect to determine the identity classification required to interpret the effect. This does not rule out causal-role individuation. If X is independently defined by a broader causal or functional role, similarity in the relevant role may justify grouping different realizations together. The problem arises only when the very outcome relation under investigation is used to bootstrap the classification needed to interpret that relation. Fourth, individuation is question-relative without being arbitrary. A variable representing “institutional power of the chair” may legitimately include veto authority and reconsideration authority as values. A variable representing “veto authority” may not. The same pair of cases can therefore form a within-variable contrast at one level and an X/X* contrast at another. This is not a verbal dispute. The level of individuation changes the causal proposition. Evidence that changing the chair’s institutional power affects adoption does not automatically establish how veto authority varies internally. Finally, the relevant boundary may be vague. If it is genuinely indeterminate whether an altered institutional capacity still counts as veto authority, the causal conclusion may need to be conditional. Under one defensible individuation, the comparison is within-X; under another, it is X/X*. Formal precision should not erase substantive uncertainty about what the variable represents. The condition thus asks for enough individuation to determine which contrast is being represented, not for a complete theory of identity. A further implication is that the same numerical coding can represent importantly different ontological contrasts. Consider a binary variable coded 1 when veto authority is present and 0 otherwise. The zero state need not be a minimal amount of an instantiated veto property. It may simply mean that no veto authority exists. By contrast, a graded variable might assign lower positive values to narrower domains or weaker blocking thresholds while preserving the underlying power. Both representations can be useful, but they answer different questions. Treating the binary zero state as though it were automatically the limiting value of a continuously instantiated property silently converts an absence contrast into a variation contrast. This matters whenever effects are summarized in a common metric. A coefficient, average treatment effect, or counterfactual difference can quantify the outcome contrast once the states have been specified, but the number does not settle what relation those states bear to the target. The same numerical difference in Y could arise from narrowing X, removing X, or replacing X with X*. Thus contrast matching is not an additional empirical variable to be estimated after the causal effect. It is part of the interpretation that tells us which effect has been estimated.6 The independence requirement should also be understood proportionately. It does not demand a source of individuation wholly insulated from all causal information. Many properties are legitimately individuated partly by their roles and effects. What is excluded is a specific circularity: using the very X-to-Y difference under dispute as the sole reason for saying that both conditions instantiate variants of X, and then using that classification to infer the X-to-Y effect. Other causalrole information, constitutive rules, functional organization, ordinary classification practices, or theoretical commitments may all contribute to a warranted individuation basis. 5. The Strongest Objection: The Model Fixes the Variable The most direct objection is that causal models already individuate their variables. If a structural model represents two conditions as values of X, then the model has specified the target. Asking for some further individuation criterion seems unnecessary and potentially hostile to legitimate scientific idealization. The objection has force because causal inference is necessarily representational. Variables are chosen for explanatory purposes, and scientific practice would be impossible if every variable required independent metaphysical certification. But the target-individuation thesis demands no such certification. The objection conflates two claims: 1. a model can define a formal variable whose values range over specified model states; 2. those states represent the worldly contrast attributed to X in the causal conclusion. The first follows from formal construction. The second does not. Suppose a model defines variable P with values: • p1: the chair possesses a veto; • p2: the chair possesses only a consultation-triggering power. As a formal variable, P is unobjectionable. If P represents “institutional powers possessed by the chair,” then both values may fit naturally within one state space. But if the result is reported as showing what happens when “veto strength” changes, an additional semantic premise is required: p2 must count as a legitimate state of veto authority rather than a distinct power. Renaming P “veto strength” cannot supply that premise. Formal continuity determines identity within the model; it does not by itself determine the worldly individuation of the represented target. The same point applies to absence conditions. A model may encode “veto strength = 0” when no veto exists. That can be a convenient formal representation. But the notation alone does not tell us whether zero is literally a value of an instantiated graded property, a coding of absence, or a boundary state introduced for modelling convenience. Different interpretations can support different verbal causal claims even when the equations are unchanged. This is closely related to the interventionist variables problem discussed by Franklin-Hall and Woodward: formal manipulability or availability does not itself determine which variable is scientifically appropriate. The present point focuses on the contrastive content of a specific causal claim. Equations operate over a state space that has already been specified; they do not, from within7 themselves, prove that the state space corresponds to within-X variation rather than X /no- X or X/X*. The result is not a rejection of modelling but a discipline of interpretation. Formal models can compare whichever states researchers find useful. The worldly conclusion must be stated at the level licensed by the interpretation of those states. 6. Measurement, Construct Validity, and Comparison Failure A deflationary response may say that this is merely a problem of construct validity. If researchers define “veto power” so broadly that it includes consultation powers, the construct is badly specified. Nothing more philosophical is needed. Construct validity can diagnose some cases, but it does not exhaust the issue. Imagine ideal measurement. Investigators perfectly identify veto authority in one condition, reconsideration authority in another, and the absence of both in a third. They know the rules, actions, and outcomes without error. No property is mismeasured. They can still draw the wrong causal proposition from the contrasts. They might interpret the veto/reconsideration comparison as “strong versus weak veto,” or the veto/absence comparison as “high versus zero realized veto,” when the substantive question is whether those descriptions fit the target being investigated. The error is not inaccurate measurement of the conditions. It is mismatch between the contrast represented and the conclusion asserted. This is especially important when constitutive conditions change. Valid measurement of X in the actual regime does not settle whether the counterfactual regime contains another state of X, no X, or a different property. That is a question about cross-condition individuation. Construct validity and target individuation therefore overlap, but the present problem is specifically contrastive and modal. Even perfectly measured cases can be related to one another under the wrong contrast. 7. Counterfactual Semantics and Contrast Matching A further objection appeals to counterfactual semantics. Possible-world theories determine relevant alternatives; interventionist approaches specify interventions; structural causal models define values and equations. Why add a separate individuation condition? Because a counterfactual framework evaluates a specified contrast; it does not automatically determine the best worldly interpretation of the terms in that contrast. Consider: If the chair’s veto were weaker, more proposals would pass. Several possible worlds may be nearby. In one, the veto applies to fewer proposals. In another, the veto is abolished. In a third, refusal merely triggers reconsideration. All may resemble the actual institution in many respects. But they represent different relations to veto authority.8 The first may be within-X variation. The second may be X /no- X. The third may be X/X*. Possible-world similarity alone does not erase these distinctions. Likewise, intervention notation such as d o ( X=x ) can encode any of them if the modeler chooses a sufficiently broad state space. The notation does not tell us whether x is another value of an instantiated X, a code for absence, or a distinct institutional state included under a superordinate variable. A counterpart theory may supply explicit criteria for relevant cross-world continuity. That would help determine the contrast rather than show that no individuation issue exists. The appropriate generalization is therefore: Counterfactual Contrast Constraint: A counterfactual inference concerning X requires a sufficiently fixed interpretation of the relation between the actual and counterfactual conditions to determine whether the contrast is within-X, X /no- X, or X/X*; the conclusion must match that interpretation. This formulation avoids the mistake of requiring X to persist in every possible world under consideration. A counterfactual can ask what would happen if X were absent. It remains a counterfactual about X because the contrast is explicitly presence versus absence. The constraint becomes restrictive only when one contrast is redescribed as another. A world in which veto authority is absent can answer what difference possessing a veto makes. It does not, without further argument, answer what happens when an existing veto is weakened. A world in which veto authority is replaced by consultation authority can answer what happens under that replacement. It does not automatically answer a within-veto question. This is a content constraint, not a prohibition on any class of counterfactuals. 8. Variation, Absence, and Replacement Effects The repaired framework distinguishes three important kinds of causal conclusion. A variation effect concerns differences among admissible states or realizations of the target: changing X from x1 to x2 changes Y . An absence effect concerns the contrast between possessing X and lacking X: having X, rather than no X, changes Y . A replacement effect concerns transition from X to a distinct X*: replacing X with X* changes Y . All three can be genuine causal claims about a research question concerning X. The restriction is not that elimination or replacement comparisons cease to be about X. It is that their results cannot automatically be converted into within-X variation claims.9 This distinction matters because identical outcome patterns can accompany all three contrasts. If proposal adoption rises by twenty percent, the numerical effect alone does not reveal whether the veto was weakened, eliminated, or replaced. The causal content depends on the represented contrast. The central inference can therefore be stated compactly: 1. A difference-making claim about X relies on a specified contrast involving X. 2. Some properties are partly individuated by constitutive background conditions. 3. Changing those conditions can generate within-X variation, absence of X, or replacement by X*. 4. Outcome differences alone cannot determine which contrast occurred. 5. Formal variable continuity cannot determine the worldly interpretation either. 6. Therefore causal and counterfactual claims about X require an independently warranted individuation sufficient to fix the relevant contrast. 7. The causal conclusion must match that contrast. The claim is not that target persistence must always be established before causal inference. Presence/absence comparisons require no instantiated persistence in the absence condition. Nor must all individuation questions be resolved before modelling begins. The stronger and more defensible point is that the interpretation must be sufficiently fixed for us to know which causal proposition the evidence bears on. 9. Philosophical Case Variation: Three Inferential Roles The same framework yields a limited metaphilosophical consequence, but only once three uses of case variation are separated. Philosophical thought experiments already have a substantial literature on identity assumptions and the evidential limits of imagined cases. Gendler (2002) examines the role of thought experiments in personal-identity reasoning; Wagner (2022) and Meier (2022) likewise address possible worlds, identity, and what thought experiments can establish in personal-identity contexts. The present claim should therefore not be that philosophy has ignored identity problems in case variation. The narrower contribution is to distinguish three inferential roles target identity can play. 9.1 Identity-testing A philosopher may alter condition B precisely to ask: does X survive without B? Here persistence is the question. It must not be assumed in advance. Suppose a theorist proposes that a particular rule is constitutive of an institutional office. We remove or alter that rule and ask whether the resulting arrangement still contains the same office. The thought experiment is designed to test the individuation claim itself. It would be question-begging to require target persistence before the case is considered. Radical hypothetical variation is therefore entirely compatible with the framework.10 9.2 Presence/absence inference A philosopher may compare a case containing X with a case lacking X in order to ask what difference possessing X makes. Here, too, persistence across both cases is unnecessary. Suppose the question is whether possessing veto authority generates a particular responsibility. One case contains the authority; another contains no such power. The comparison can genuinely concern veto authority even though the second case lacks it. The relevant burden is simply to interpret the contrast correctly as X /no- X. The same is true in moral, political, or epistemic examples. An argument may legitimately compare an agent with a capacity to an agent without it, or an institution with a norm to one without it. No mistake follows merely from absence. 9.3 Persistence-dependent downstream inference The philosophically delicate case is different. A philosopher alters B, treats the modified case as still containing X, and then uses intuitions about the modified case to infer some further property P of X. Here target persistence functions as a bridge premise. The structure is: 1. actual case: X exists under B; 2. modify or remove B; 3. treat the modified case as still a case of X; 4. infer from that case whether X has property P. If step 3 is necessary for the inference to concern X, then the persistence claim must have independent warrant. Otherwise the argument may have changed subjects before reaching its conclusion about P. This is the precise scope of the metaphilosophical constraint. 10. The Objection from Philosophical Flexibility The strongest objection is that philosophy often changes apparently constitutive conditions precisely to discover whether they really are constitutive. If target persistence must be independently warranted whenever a case changes such conditions, thought experiments would become conservative. Whatever we initially regarded as essential could never be challenged. The three-way distinction shows why this objection does not defeat the framework. In an identity-testing thought experiment, persistence is not a bridge premise. It is the object of inquiry. The philosopher is entitled to leave open whether the modified case still contains X. The problem arises only when the modified case is used for a different purpose. Suppose a philosopher removes condition B, assumes that X survives, and then uses the modified case to infer11 whether X possesses property P. In that downstream argument, persistence is doing substantive work. If removing B actually eliminates X or produces X*, the intuition about the modified case may concern absence or replacement rather than the original target. Consider again an institutional example. A philosopher may remove a blocking rule specifically to test whether that rule is constitutive of veto authority. No prior answer about persistence is needed. Alternatively, the philosopher may compare a veto case with a no-veto case to determine what difference possessing veto authority makes. Again, persistence in both cases is unnecessary. But suppose the philosopher removes the blocking rule, continues to call the remaining consultation power “the veto,” and uses intuitions about the holder’s obligations in that modified case to infer the obligations of veto holders generally. Now the argument requires the consultation case to remain a case of veto authority. That requirement is a bridge premise, not an innocent description. The logical distinction can be made explicit. An identity-testing argument asks: alter B; does X remain? A presence/absence argument asks: compare X with no X; what difference does possessing X make? A persistence-dependent downstream argument asks: alter B; treat the result as still X; what does this show about further property P of X? Only the third requires persistence to be independently warranted as a condition of the downstream inference. This leaves philosophy free to use radical cases, revise concepts, and test proposed identity conditions. The framework does not demand metaphysical conservatism. It demands only that the inferential role assigned to persistence be made explicit. The bridge-premise formulation also clarifies what follows when persistence is uncertain rather than false. Suppose the modified case might still instantiate X, but the issue is genuinely contested. The downstream argument need not be abandoned. Its conclusion can be conditional: if the modified case preserves X, then the case supplies evidence about P; if it instead removes or replaces X, that evidential route is blocked or changes content. This is philosophically preferable to smuggling the disputed identity claim into the case description. Conversely, a philosopher need not defend persistence merely because the same noun appears in both descriptions. Natural language often allows us to speak loosely of “the institution,” “the role,” or “the capacity” after major transformations. Such continuity of description can be useful for tracking a scenario, but it should not be confused with the substantive continuity required by an inference about a specific property. The methodological question is always what the conclusion needs the case to preserve. 11. Why the Three-Way Distinction Matters The three-way distinction prevents two opposite errors.12 The first is over-demanding persistence. If every causal or philosophical comparison about X were required to instantiate X in all conditions, ordinary presence/absence reasoning would become illegitimate. That is clearly too strong. We often learn what difference a property makes by comparing cases with and without it. The second is under-demanding persistence. Once a modified case is used to infer a further property of a supposedly continuing target, it matters whether the case still concerns that target. Treating persistence as automatic can convert evidence about absence or replacement into evidence about within-target variation. The framework therefore gives identity different methodological roles in different arguments. In identity-testing, identity is the dependent question. In presence/absence inference, identity fixes which property is present in one case and absent in another, but does not require instantiation on both sides. In persistence-dependent downstream inference, identity is a bridge premise connecting the modified case to a further conclusion about the same target. This asymmetry is more precise than the general slogan that “identity matters.” It explains why some case variations legitimately suspend persistence while others cannot. It also clarifies the relationship between replacement and variation. Replacement is not an inferential failure. An X/X* contrast can be exactly the comparison we need. The failure occurs when its conclusion is reported as though the evidence had come from an X1 / X2 contrast. Likewise, elimination is not replacement. An X /no- X contrast does not require a substitute X*. It can directly answer what difference possessing X makes. The discipline is therefore one of contrast matching, not persistence at all costs. 12. Scope and Limits The thesis should not be extended beyond what this argument earns. It does not require strict numerical identity across all counterfactual states. Depending on the subject, the relevant continuity may involve type identity, role continuity, common realization conditions, or an appropriate counterpart relation. It does not deny vague or contested boundaries. If it is genuinely indeterminate whether a transformed case instantiates X, a causal or philosophical conclusion may need to remain conditional on competing individuation schemes. It does not prohibit broad or disjunctive variables. An inquiry may deliberately study a category broad enough to include vetoes, consultation powers, and other forms of institutional control. The conclusion must then be stated at that level. It does not require an explicit metaphysical inquiry before every causal model is constructed. Individuation, modelling, and explanatory purpose can develop together. The claim is only that formal equations cannot by themselves determine whether the worldly contrast represented is withinX, X /no- X, or X/X*.13 Finally, it does not yield a general theory of thought experiments. The metaphilosophical result is narrower: when a modified case is used to infer a further property of a supposedly persisting target, and that inference depends on the case still being a case of X, persistence functions as a substantive bridge premise. If persistence itself is under test, it must remain open. If the comparison is presence versus absence, persistence in both cases is unnecessary. This limited result is enough. It identifies where identity is constitutive of the inference and where it is not. 13. Conclusion Difference-making reasoning does not require every comparison condition to instantiate the same target. A legitimate inquiry about X may compare one value of X with another, one realization with another, the presence of X with its absence, or X with a specified replacement X*. What matters is that the contrast represented by the cases match the conclusion drawn from them. This requirement becomes important when interventions or counterfactual antecedents alter conditions partly constitutive of the target. Such changes can preserve X, eliminate X, or replace it with X*. Outcome differences alone cannot determine which has occurred. Formal continuity of a model variable cannot determine it either, because a single symbol can encode within-target variation, absence, or heterogeneous replacement states. The appropriate requirement is therefore not universal target persistence but individuation consistency. A comparison concerning X needs a defensible basis sufficient to determine what relation each condition bears to X, and the causal or counterfactual conclusion must be stated at that level. This yields three distinct kinds of result. Within-target comparisons support variation claims. Presence/absence comparisons can support claims about the effect of possessing X. Replacement comparisons can support claims about replacing X with X*. None is inherently inferior. The error is to infer one kind of proposition from evidence generated by another kind of contrast without further argument. The same distinction sharpens philosophical case reasoning. When a thought experiment tests whether X survives a change, persistence is the question and must not be assumed. When it compares X with no X, persistence in both cases is unnecessary. But when a modified case is treated as still containing X and then used to infer some further property P of X, target persistence functions as a bridge premise. If that bridge is unsupported, the downstream inference may have changed subjects. The deepest point is therefore not that variation always requires persistence. It is that differencemaking requires the right contrast. A comparison tells us what difference X makes only when we know whether we have varied X, removed X, or replaced X—and when our conclusion says no more than that contrast can support.
