Who Gets to Say an AI System Is Safe?

AI governance needs more than evaluations and participation. Evidence, legitimacy, authority, execution, and outcome validation must form one accountable loop.

Back to Public Notes繁體中文 ↗

AI governance is rapidly gaining two capabilities: better technical evaluation and broader public participation. Yet even when both are present, governance can still fail to close.

Knowing what happened is not the same as deciding who has standing to act. Issuing a decision is not the same as proving that the deployed system actually changed.

Evidence ≠ Legitimacy ≠ Authority ≠ Execution ≠ Outcome Validation.

This note begins from Audrey Tang’s 28 October 2026 Stanford HAI keynote and fireside conversation. The governance loop developed below is a MirrorTone Lab research synthesis; it should not be read as a framework proposed verbatim by Tang.

1. Start with the question: who sets the pace?

Stanford HAI’s 2026 conference was titled Confronting Our AI Future: Hope, Fear, and the Choices Ahead ↗. Tang opened with Humanity Sets the Pace of AI, followed by a fireside conversation with Rob Reich.

In the public transcript, she organizes the proposal around three working parts: evidence that anyone can check, a witness standing outside the race and resistant to capture, and a duty to answer publicly by a deadline. This moves “pace” away from a vague choice between acceleration and pause toward institutional questions: who can inspect the evidence, who can judge whether it is sufficient, and who must answer to that judgment? See the public transcript ↗.

2. Evidence can be verified, but evidence cannot authorize itself

The first layer is evidence. High-stakes AI evaluation has a structural trust problem when the developer controls what is tested, what outsiders may see, and what becomes public.

In August 2026, Google DeepMind, the Singapore AI Safety Institute, OpenMined, AVERI, and MLCommons reported a double-blind evaluation of Gemini 2.5 Flash-Lite. Evaluators did not receive the model weights; the model provider did not receive the confidential test prompts; execution occurred inside a protected computing environment. The pilot showed that external evaluation need not always require either the model owner or the evaluator to surrender its most sensitive asset. See Google DeepMind ↗ and OpenMined ↗.

But even perfect evaluation would answer an epistemic question only: what reasons do we have to believe a capability or risk claim? It does not automatically answer the political question: is that risk socially acceptable, and who has standing to decide for those who bear it?

3. Public legitimacy is not a substitute for technical expertise

Tang and Yale political theorist Hélène Landemore have proposed a standing citizens’ assembly of roughly one thousand people selected by lot across the world, rotating over time. Its role is not to replace safety researchers or run model evaluations. It is to create a public witness that is not structurally inside one lab or one state. See Landemore & Tang, Noema, 23 September 2026 ↗.

Taiwan provides a smaller-scale example. In 2024, the Ministry of Digital Affairs recruited through 200,000 random SMS invitations; 447 citizens ultimately deliberated in 44 groups on AI and information integrity. The institutional value of such a process is not that citizens suddenly become better AI engineers. It is that risk tolerance, public priorities, and affected people gain a formal place in the decision structure. See the Ministry of Digital Affairs ↗.

Legitimacy ≠ Expertise.

Experts can answer what a system can do and what a test observed. The public and affected communities must retain a real role in deciding which consequences are unacceptable and which risks deserve priority. These roles are complementary; neither should impersonate the other.

4. A duty to answer matters—but an answer is not yet a consequence

Taiwan’s JOIN public-participation platform offers a clear institutional prototype: a proposal that reaches 5,000 endorsements within 60 days becomes an official case, and the responsible agency must provide a substantive public response within two months. See the JOIN platform ↗.

This is much stronger than “we heard you,” because responsibility is attached to a named institution, a deadline, and a public answer. But it also exposes the next gap:

Duty to answer is not yet authority to constrain.

A company may explain in detail why it disagrees and then continue deployment unchanged. The load-bearing question therefore becomes: what kind of evidence, at what level of risk, may legitimately trigger what kind of consequence?

This need not imply a universal stop mechanism. Consequences can range from public explanation and mitigation to re-evaluation, deployment constraints, or regulatory intervention. The point is not to weaken AI; it is to prevent capability from silently generating its own authority.

5. The missing layer is authority

At least five layers must remain distinct:

Evidence: what do we know, and can it be independently checked?

Legitimacy: who bears the consequences, and who has standing to set acceptable risk and public priorities?

Authority: who is lawfully or institutionally empowered to require a change, restrict deployment, or impose a stop?

Execution: was the requirement actually implemented rather than merely announced?

Outcome Validation: after implementation, did the risk actually fall, and can the original failure still be reproduced?

A recurring governance error is to let one layer impersonate the next: a report becomes “governance”; consultation becomes “legitimacy”; a policy becomes “execution”; a patch becomes “problem solved.”

6. Evaluated Is Not Deployed

There is also a more technical discontinuity. In the Stanford talk, Tang notes that the double-blind evaluation pilot is not yet the production system. That difference is not a minor implementation detail.

A deployed AI system may include system prompts, tool permissions, retrieval, memory, routing, agent scaffolding, post-training, and changing model weights. A material change to any of these can separate the thing that was evaluated from the thing that is now operating.

Evaluated ≠ Deployed ≠ Current ≠ Still Compliant.

Governance therefore needs more than a one-time safety evaluation. It needs deployment identity and continuous compliance: the current operating system should be traceable to the identity that was evaluated; material changes should trigger proportionate re-evaluation; and corrective action should be followed by readback of the real system state.

This is one of the most consequential gaps between “safety evidence” and “current reality.”

7. A more complete governance loop

Connecting the layers produces not a new central ruler, but a loop in which responsibility cannot evaporate:

Evidence → Public Judgment → Authority → Action → Runtime Readback → Correction.

Evaluators produce evidence; citizens and affected communities contribute legitimacy and risk priorities; lawful institutions hold consequential authority; developers execute changes; independent readback checks whether reality changed; new evidence re-enters correction.

The purpose is not to create more bureaucracy. It is to prevent one actor from monopolizing problem definition, evidence production, safety declaration, deployment choice, and completion declaration at the same time.

8. Human sovereignty does not require everyone to operate the model

“Humans retain final authority” can be misunderstood to mean that everyone must understand frontier models, personally evaluate them, or place laboratories under direct citizen control. None of that is required.

A more workable principle is: technical capability can be highly specialized, but consequential authority must not arise automatically from specialization.

Society allows a small number of people to design aircraft without allowing aircraft manufacturers to define the airspace rules, accident standards, and whether an incident has been fully resolved. Powerful AI can likewise be built by specialized teams without allowing evidence, acceptable-risk boundaries, deployment authority, and outcome validation to remain closed inside the same organization.

Conclusion | Governance is not evaluation completed; it is accountability closed

Tang’s Stanford proposal makes an important move: it converts the question of frontier AI “pace” from an abstract argument about speed into three institutional mechanisms—checkable evidence, a non-capturable witness, and a duty to answer.

We argue that two further load-bearing layers are required: Authority and Outcome Validation.

A system is not governed merely because it has been evaluated. Governance begins when evidence, legitimate authority, action, and outcome can be traced as one accountable loop.

The goal of AI governance is not to make AI weaker. It is to ensure that increasing capability cannot bypass evidence, public judgment, lawful authority, real execution, and outcome readback while expanding its power.
